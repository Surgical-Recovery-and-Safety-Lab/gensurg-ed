Antibiotic Prophylaxis in Surgery

Influences on infection rate

•    ASA / APACHE II scores

•    Duration of surgery beyond 2 hrs

•    Degree of Wound Contamination

Reducing Infection Rates

Do not shave early or razor shave (depilate or clip immediately before operating)

Antibiotic Prophylaxis

Undoubtedly useful in procedures where contamination readily predictable, e.g. colon resection

Also in high-risk patients undergoing gastric surgery: risk factors such as malignancy, concurrent acid reduction make a normally low-bacteria area possibly contaminated.

In appendicectomy, the organ is already infected, so it is not strictly prophylactic use.  Remember the major organisms are likely to be obligate anaerobes.

In biliary operations, there is close concordance between infected bile and post-op sepsis complications, e.g. wound infection.  Note that cultures more often positive in acute cholecystitis, jaundice, choledochal stones, DM, non-functioning gallbladder, or age >70, but cultures are nonetheless insensitive.  Do not therefore restrict antibiotic use: use prophylactic antibiotics in all patients undergoing biliary surgery

Prophylaxis is used in all foreign body implants, eg vascular, cardiac, orthopaedic implants: often have severe complications in the event of infection. 

Prophylaxis could also be extended to other operations, eg hernia, breast, CNS, because infection can lead to disability and stress, sometimes requiring another operation.

Invasive investigations with a high rate of bacteraemia also need cover, eg endoscopy, percutaneous abscess drainage, abdo exploration in penetrating trauma, chest tubes in chest trauma.

Principles:

•    High concentration in target tissue at incision time and until time of wound closure

•    IV administration ideally at time of induction, ie 20 mins prior to incision.

•    Oral regimen timing is critical: must reach maximum levels at skin site in time

•    If lasting <2 hrs, a single dose is adequate.  Additional doses after would closure have no evidence of benefit.

•    Procedures lasting >2 hrs (or if pronounced blood loss) may need one-two further doses at intervals 2x plasma half life.  A final dose may be given just prior to wound closure.

•    Antibiotics chose on basis of most likely microbes to infect the site, and always S. aureus.

•    In emergency, active infection, obstruction, unusual blood loss, trauma, high-risk patients or other special circumstances, may prolong the regimen (ie therapy, not prophylaxis)

 

Examples

•    Appendicectomy (anaerobes, coliforms): cefotetan or ciprofloxacin or ceftriaxone and metronidazole.

•    Biliary (coliforms, enterococci, anaerobes in obstruction): coamoxyclav or cephazolin or ceftriaxone.

•    Cardiovascular (staph): cefamandole or teicoplanin.

•    Colorectal (anaerobes, coliforms): cefotetan or coamoxyclav or cefuroxime and metronidazole.

•    Gastric malignancy (coliforms, anaerobes): coamoxyclav or cephazolin and metronidazole.

•    Gynaecological (coliforms, anaerobes, streptococci): cefotetan or coamoxyclav.

•    Orthopaedic hip (staph, coliforms): cephazolin or cefamandole or teicoplanin.

•    Orthopaedic amputation (clostridia, staph, strep): coamoxyclav or flucloxacillin +/- metronidazole.

•    Transplant (coliforms, pseudomonads, staphylococci): ciprofloxacin, ceftazidime.

•    Urological (coliforms): coamoxyclav, gentamicin.
