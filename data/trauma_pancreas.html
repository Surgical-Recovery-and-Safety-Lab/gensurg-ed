Pancreatic Trauma
DEFINITION
Trauma - pancreas
D E A B M I M
EPIDEMIOLOGY

Incidence
Uncommon; 1-4% abdo injuries only.

D E A B M I M
AETIOLOGY

Trauma

Blunt
Usually deceleration, forcing structures against spine
- neck is at risk of transection across the vertebrae.

Penetrating
Usually associated injuries to adjacent structures.

D E A B M I M
BIOLOGICAL BEHAVIOUR

Pathophysiology
Retroperitoneal location has tamponading effect; often not assoc. with major bleeding

Complications
Pancreatic fistula & pseudocysts (6%)
Pancreatitis (2%)
Abscesses (5%)


D E A B M I M
MANIFESTATIONS

Often serial examinations are unreliable due to retroperitoneal site.
Pain may be indicative in delayed diagnoses.
Usually CT detected.
D E A B M I M

INVESTIGATIONS

Biochemistry
Do not trust initial hyperamalysaemia for diagnosis
- elevated in 40% of pts with trauma secondary to alcohol intoxication.
Rising amylase over 24-48 hrs is strongly suggestive of pancreas injury.

Imaging
Contrast CT with a 64-slice scanner = modality of choice.
Triple contrast for penetrating injuries especially.

Diagnostic features:
- parenchymal haematoma or lac
- transection with fluid in lesser sac
- disruption of head
- diffuse swelling (post-pancreatitis)

Suggestive (associated) features:
- haematoma of transverse mesocolon
- thick L anterior renal fascia
- fluid in lesser sac or between pancreas and splenic vein
- duodenal injury
- L kidney, adrenal or spleen injury
- Chance # of lumbar spine.

Pitfalls (making panc. trauma difficult to see)
- minimal extraperiotoneal fat
- artifacts from dense oral contrastr
- nonopactification of duodenum or small bowel.

If CT equivocal, and amylase rise suggestive, then ERCP is appropriate.

Grading
I = minor haematoma / lac
II = major haematoma / lac
III = distal transection or duct injury
IV = proximal transection or involving ampulla
V = massive head disruption

MANAGEMENT

Principles
Operative management determined by:
- magnitude of injury
- presence of associated injury
- hemodynamic / metabolic status

Conservative
Grade I, II = conservative
- in otherwise uncompromised pts.
- rarely may lead to pseudocysts, but unlikely.
Follow up CT in 5-7d, for Grade II
- or if symptoms / signs develop

Operative

 Aim to evaluate the gland for injury and particularly ductal injury.

Approach
Not examined in damage control laparotomies
Otherwise:
- Kocherise to see head,
- divide gastrocolic omentum to see body
- elevate spleen to see tail injuries.
--> explore haematomas.

Grade I-II injuries
Can tack a small piece of omentum onto the laceration
- plugs small leaks and brings macrophages to the area

Duct injury?
If duct injury away from head, will see extensive fat necrosis in the lesser sac.
- control local bleeding, watch for 3-5 m for fluid leak; can inject secretin to enhance this.

Controlling Duct Leak Surgery
In tail, body or neck:
- distal pancreatectomy, preferably with splenic salvage if young.
Splenic salvage:
- place fine ties on the vein/artery side, and clips on the pancreas (resected) side.
No salvage:
- suture-ligate with 4-0 polypropylene suture 2cm proximal to pancreas site.
- then cover these sutures with an omental pedicle
--> isolates vessels from a stump leak.
- staples are used on the proximal pancreas (3.5mm or 4.8mm if thick) as suturing can cause necrosis.
- no clear-cut data that suturing the duct prevents leaks from this site.

Pancreaticojejunostomy?
In patients with transections, who are very stable, another option is distal Roux-en-Y pancreaticojejunostomy.
- complete proximal transection, oversew proximal end
- perform p-j
- stent duct, through 5cm distal to jejunostomy, through abdo wall, to reduce fistulas.
- stent goes 2cm, into dict and is secured with a 3-0 absorbable suture.

Whipples?
Consider if macerated head or descruction of ampulla.
- may be possible immediately in stable patients.
- else pack for damage control and suction drains
Witzel jejunostomy; used once midgut oedema resolved.
