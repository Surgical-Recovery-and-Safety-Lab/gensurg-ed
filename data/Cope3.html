Cope 3 : Methods of Diagnosis - Examination
1.  Take time to watch the patient quietly.  Is their face belying colicky pain instead of steady pain as they may state?  Pallor, sweating, dehydration, clammy etc etc.

2.  The attitude in bed.  Knees are often drawn up in peritonitis. Sitting in pancreatitis vs lying flat in peritonitis.

3. "High fever is quite unusual during the early stages of acute abdominal disease except for severe pancreatitis".    Hypothermia is possible in shock.  Ruptured ectopics and the like are usually accompanied by irregular low-grade fever.

4.  Many lives are lost from failure to examine all the hernial orifices, especially the femoral canal in an obese patient.

5.  Watch the respiratory motion of the abdominal wall.  Usually immobile over peritonism, cf freely moving in pneumonia with abdominal pleuritic pain, for instance.

6.  Rough palpation by the first examiner usually makes the pt fearful and can mislead subsequent examiners.

7.  "It is well to have the patient's legs flexed and supported in flexion during examination of the abdomen."

8.  Gentle percussion is advised over rebound, which is an unnecessary and unexpected pain.

9.  Rigidity is relative.  Board-like abdomens are less-frequently observed than generally believed.  In sensitive souls the abdomen can be held tense by apprehension; can force them to behave by placing pillows under knees and maybe chest, then putting a left hand with pressure on their sternum, inducing diaphragmatic breathing.  "In rigidity due to thoracic disease, if the muscle contraction is overcome by continuous pressure, the abdo pain is not usually increased".    Remember muscles can be thin and weak, abdo walls fat and flabby, reflexes may be dulled by toxaemia, and especially that the elderly may be too feeble.  Usually is more pronounced laterally.

10.  Hyperaesthesia.  Test the loin, lumbar and thigh regions cf abdo wall.  Occurs along peripheral nerve zones whose terminals irritated by abdominal inflammation. 

11. Note on PR: Push against the prostate/cervix then against the pelvic peritoneum to determine if the discomfort is due to pressure of the fingertip or peritonism.

12. Thigh rotation test.  When an inflamed mass sits against the obturator muscle.  Flex the thigh, and internally rotate it; hypogastric pain will result.

13. Auscultation must not be given undue weight.  Rarely should it be important by itself.  Chest peristalsis does not indicated diaphragmatic hernia.  Abdo bruit is common in the elderly but is not sensitive of anything.
