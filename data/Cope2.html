Cope 2 : Methods of Diagnosis - The History
1.  "One can gain an enormous amount of information from watching the patient.  For that reason I always elicit the history while sitting at the bedside with the patient's abdomen bared.   The patient's attitude in bed, the appearance of the facies during paroxysms of pain, and changes in contour of the abdomen may be extremely informative".

2.  "There is surely no greater wisdom than to time the beginning and onsets of things" (Bacon).  A large number of acute abdominal conditions can be diagnosed by considering carefully the history of their onset.  That is only possible, however, when each symptom is carefully appraised in relation to other symptoms, so that its significance is properly understood.  This is of greatest use when the chronological appearance of symptoms, from the time of last wellbeing, is meticulously recorded.  More diagnoses will be made by this means than by CT scan.

3.  Age.

4.  Exact time and mode of onset.  Awakening from sleep is usually of great significance.  Aperients may make pain worse, eg appendicitis.  Straining may rupure the ectopic, or the ulcer.    Apparantly trivial injuries may accompany serious lesions, eg the spleen.  What were they doing when the pain begun?

5.  Pain : acuteness.  Fainting at pain onset is serious: perf ulcer, acute pancreatitis, ruptured AAA.  Intestinal obstruction is gradual, but strangulation is often very acute from the first.  Torsion of an ovarian pedicle are also acute from the start.  Ask about time of onset: it is unusual for rigors / high fevers within 24hrs of appendicitis - or perforation; rigors and high fevers within 24hrs almost excludes appendicitis.

6.  Pain : situation.  Flooding with blood, or pus, or acrid fluid causes generalised pain : but often maximum in the upper in ulcers, and lower in blood / pus.  Look for change in pain, as classically is the case in appendicitis (the appendix nerves having the same origin as small bowel); or as in dissection aneurism, beginning in the thorax and progressing to the abdomen.

7.  Pain : character.  Examples: general burning (perf DU), agonising (acute pancreatitis), sharp constricting pain (biliary colic), tearing pain (dissection), griping (obstruction), acute aching (appendix).

8.  Pain : radiation.  Sometimes diagnostic, especially for colics.  Ascertain if pain is influenced by respiration : pleuritic? resp inhibited (eg in gallbladder pain)? worsens pain (peritonitis, obstruction)?

9.  Pain : special varieties.  Eg pain on micturition (pyelitis, stone, acute hydronephrosis, pelvic appendix).  Positional pain.

10.  Vomiting.  Either severe pelvic irritation (perf DU, gangrenous appendix, ovarian torsion), or obstruction of a muscular tube (biliary duct, ureter, uterine canal, intestine, appendix).
- in a perf'd ulcer, vomiting is usual soon after rupture, but abates as dilution of gastric contents occurs.
- sympathetic stimulation (severe) as in torsion or strangulation may cause acute vomiting.
- profuse in obstruction, where antiperistalsis and impairment of absorption contribute.
- vomiting is non-specific and non-sensitive.
- anorexia is part of the spectrum; if questioning a child, ask what the last meal they actually enjoyed was, and 'the first for which they had no eagerness'.

11.  Pain & Vomiting : relation.  Sudden and severe stimulation causes early vomiting.  The site of intestinal obstruction can be determined by the time to onset of vomiting (minutes to hours).   Pain almost always preceedes vomiting in appendicitis, sometimes by 12-24 hours or longer.  Rarely simultaneous; very rarely before pain.

12.  Frequency of Vomiting.  Vomiting at early onset implies a blocked and distended appendix; signals possible early perforation.  In acute pancreatitis, vomiting is frequent but often slight. 

13. Type of Vomit.  Gastritis may have a little bile.  Colic is frequently bilious, and feculant vomiting is pathognomonic for distal small bowel obstruction (rare in colonic). 

14.  Bowels.  Departure from normal is important.  Small, loose motions coincides with appendicitis in children.  When hypogastric pain and diarrhoea are followed by hypogastric tenderness and constipation, suspect a pelvic abscess.
-  Profuse watery diarrhoea is extremely ocmmon in partial bowel obstruction.  Presence of obvious blood and mucus in the motions should be determined. 

15.  Menstruation.  Not sufficient to ask whether the periods are regular; exact date should be ascertained and any irregularity noted.  Even if at the right time, pathological bleeding is usually abnormal.
