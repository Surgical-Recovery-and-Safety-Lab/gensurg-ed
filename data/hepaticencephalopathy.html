Hepatic Encephalopathy
DEFINITION

A potentially reversible neuropsychiatric syndrome seen in patients with cirrhosis or acute liver failure.

BIOLOGICAL BEHAVIOUR

Pathophysiology

 Multifactorial and not fully elucidated.
Toxic effects on brain from substances produced by GI bacterial action on nitrogenous substances.
These are normally cleared by the liver, which cannot occur as compromised.
Ammonia best studied agent.
- e.g. crosses BBB, enters astrocytes, combines with glutamate to alter neurotransmitters; damages / swells astrocytes.
- also effects on cerebral perfusion and glucose metabolism
Also increased GABA, altered neurotransmitter effects.

Classification

A. Encephalopathy and acute liver failure
B. Encephalopathy and portosystemic bypass; no intrinsic hepatocellular disease
C. Encephalopathy with cirrhosis and portal hypertension

Precipitating Factors
GI bleeding (most common)
- decreased perfusion of liver and kidneys
- and large protein load from blood auto-digestion
Sepsis
- common concurrent problem
- may be astrocytic swelling from cytokines
Sedatives and analgesics
Dehydration and renal failure
Hypokalemia
Metabolic alkalosis
Excessive dietary protein
Constipation

Natural history
Can be precipitated by inter-current illness or spontaneous
Can be recurrent / relapsing / persistent

INVESTIGATIONS

Largely a clinical diagnosis.
Exclude low glucose, intoxication

Biochemistry
High ammonia common but not universal and does not correlate with severity.

Other
EGG always abnormal when overt but not usually necessary.

MANAGEMENT

Medical

1. Search carefully for latent infection?
Note cirrhotic patients may have baseline neutropaenia from hypersplenism
- may not demonstrate a leukocytosis in sepsis.
- also may be hypothermic if liver is bad, and may not mount fever.
Paracentesis in all patients with ascites to exclude SBP

2. Consider empiric antibiotics
- avoiding aminoglycosides

3. Optimise fluid balance and electrolytes
- may be on potent diuretics for cirrhosis.
- renal hypoperfusion may increase urea and worsen ammonia production
- hypokalaemic alkalosis will heighten renal ammonia production

4. Avoid sedatives and analgesics
- especially benzos

5. Limit dietary protein
Theoretically reduces nitrogenous toxin production
Lacks clear evidence from controlled trials.
Challenging as often patients are malnourished and would benefit from protein
- accelerating catabolism by withholding protein can worsen the problem
Restrict initially then modestly limit to 1 g/kg/day

6. Lactulose
A mainstay of therapy despite Cochrane review showing no established evidence.
Increases ammonia clearance (theoretically) by cathartic action and decreases ammonia absorption by trapping in colonic lumen.
Perhaps best in bleeders, promotes exit of blood from tract
Oral, or if unable, rectal retention enema (300 mL in 700 mL water)
Give to persistent patients sufficient to produce 3-5 motions / day; e.g. 30mL bd

7. Other
Sodium benzoate promising in limited trials
Possible role for probiotics

Note on Type A (Associated Acute Liver Failure)

Problematic associated cerebral oedema
Related to fluid balance and cytokines
- avoid intravenous hypotonic solutions
Intensive care if grade 2; ventilation if grade 3 for airway protection
Mannitol (0.5-1 g/kg) if objectively elevated ICP
- and temporary hyperventilation aiming for PCO2 of 25.
Renal replacement therapy to lower ammonia
- lactulose less effective in acute liver failure
Non-responders should be considered for transplant.
