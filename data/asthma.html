ASTHMA

DEFINITION
Episodic attacks of coughing, wheezing and difficulty breathing, due to narrowing of the air passages by inflammation and excessive mucus production, in response to multiplicity of stimuli.

INCIDENCE
Prevalence
Children 10-20%, adults 10%.
Increasing in West, also severity increasing.
Age
Any, but predominantly early life (1/2 before 10 yrs, rare <6 months).
Can occur de novo in adulthood.
Sex
Childhood M2:1F.
Equalises by 30 years.

Risk factors
FHx of asthma/ atopy increases risk.

AETIOLOGY

Heterogenous but two categories
Allergenic
Typical.
Inflammatory hypersensitivity - causally related in 1/3, contribute in another 1/3.
Idiosyncratic
Seemingly not related to hypersensitivity.
Often incited by URTI
- young commonly resp syncitial virus, para-influenza virus.
- older kids / adults commonly rhinovirus and influenza virus.

Age relation
- Younger patients tend to allergic, older idiosyncratic.
- Many patients in-between.

Other
Aspirin-induced
- also some colourings and beta-adrenergic antagonists.
- possibly decrease prostaglandins and increase leukotrienes.
Occupational
- allergic or toxic irritation stimulated bronchoconstriction.
- e.g. metal salts, wood, pollution, veggie dusts, some pharmaceuticals, some plastics and other chemicals.

BIOLOGICAL BEHAVIOUR
An episodic disease with periods of wellness interspersed by periods of illness.

Natural History

Inciting agents
Viruses - especially in infants and aged.
Inhaled allergens - especially children to adults.
Aspirin.
Occupational.
GORD in elderly.

In childhood
75% mild episodic asthma, 20% frequent episodic, 5% persistent.
- 2/3 remission in adulthood, less likely if permanent change (see below), or more severe.
Mortality is uncommon - have been drug issues in past with beta agonists.
Worse prognosis if socially deprived.

Phases
Acute - activation, degranulation, mediators cause bronchoconstriction, oedema, mucous plugging.
Chronic - cell recruitment, epithelial damage, structural change.
Remodelling - cellular proliferation, narrowing of airway.
Treatments target these 3 areas.

Pathogenesis - Allergic

Genetic and environmental factors sensitise to allergens.
Must be abundant in the environment, often seasonally.

On first exposure:
Cytokines  released from Th cells
--> activate B cells to produce IgE
--> stimulate mast cells and eosinophils to divide.
--> much IgE is produced against the allergen.
I- gE levels rise in minutes, can remain high for weeks - hence obstructions for considerable time after just 1 exposure.

On subsequent exposure
Acute hypersensitivity response.
--> allergen bound by IgE on mast cells airway mucosa
--> mast cell degranulation.
--> chemical mediators (e.g. histamine, leukotrienes) released from mast cells
--> increase vessel permeability -> oedema of the mucosa.
--> increase mucus production.
--> stimulate subepithelial vagal receptors provoking bronchoconstriction.
--> narrow bronchi and hence the acute response.
The mediators also include chemotactic factors which attract further inflammatory cells.
--> sets up the late phase reaction (4-8 hours later).

Chronic exposure
Chronic inflammation
--> airway remodelling with progressive damage to epithelium, subepithelial fibrosis and hypertrophy of smooth muscle
--> permanent airway obstruction.

Pathogenesis - Idiosyncratic

Underlying bronchial hyper-responsiveness
- excessive tendency of airways to narrow to non-specific stimuli

URTIs can trigger paroxysms of wheezing and dyspnoea.
- viral inflammation may lower threshold of subepithelial vagal receptors to irritants, even in non-asthmatics.
Exercise is also a common precipitants.
- provokes bronchospasm.
- proportional to exertion, coldness of air.
Stress - in up to half asthmatics
- may trigger disease or relaxation may ameliorate.
- ?vagus or endorphin related.

Pathophysiology

Increased airway resistance.
Decreased flow rates.
Decreased FEV.
Premature airway closure.
Static & dynamic hyperinflation.
Increased work of breathing.

Gases
Impaired diffusion, not uniformly -> V/Q mismatch (perfusion of inadequately ventilated alveoli).
If mild less PaO2, less PaCO2.
If moderate, lots less PaO2, normal PaCO2.
If severe, heaps less PaO2, more PaCO2.

Nerves
Nerves involved are parasympathetic (contraction, Ach mediated) & Non-adrenergic, non-cholinergic (NANC - relaxation, NO & possibly VIP mediated).
No sympathetic contact, but perhaps role of circulating adrenal medulla agents.

MANIFESTATIONS

Symptoms
Hospital presentations often preceeded by deteriorating control.

Local
Paroxysmal episodes of wheeze, dry cough and breathlessness/ constriction in chest lasting minutes to hours.
Night cough is common.
Often triggered by exercise, dust, pollens, cats, cold air, emotion or infection.
Status asthmaticus is a condition in which the attacks do not remit and there is a permanent degree of airway narrowing.
If non resolving, resp failure with drowsiness, confusion,  coma, death.

Signs
Observe
Difficulty breathing
Tachypnoea
Prolonged expiration.
Increased anterior-posterior diameter (due to gas trapping by mucus plugs).
Accessory muscle use.
At termination of attack cough up thick stringy mucus.
Cyanosis is a late sign.
Signs of Atopy
- rhinitis, sinusitis, flexural eczema, nasal polyps.
Harrison's sulcus
- linear depression of lower ribs just above the costal margin where diaphragm inserts (if severe childhood asthma)

Auscultate
Wheeze - if mild heard only at end expiration - usually polyphonic and high-pitched.
Expiratory slowing.
Silent chest is a sign of very severe asthma with no air flowing.

Assessment
Asthma deaths are preventable.
Assess severity and treat early.

Mild-moderate
Can speak in sentences.
May be some indrawing.
Wheeze (esp on forced expiration).
PEF still >60% of predicted.

Moderate - severe
Speaking in words.
Marked indrawing and accessory muscle use.
Audible wheeze.
PEF40-60% of normal.
HR>110
Resps>25
Pulsus paradoxus 10-20mmHg (weak with inspiration, strong with expiration - due to collapsing pulmonary vessels?).

Life-threatening
- presence of any 1 sign = take note.
Unable to talk.
Exhausted.
Silent chest.
Impaired consciousness.
Cyanosis
PEF<40% normal.
Pulsus paradoxus >20 mmHg.
Hypotension

INVESTIGATIONS
Spirometry
Decreased peak flow and decreased flow overall.
Should be reversible with administration of a bronchodilator (unless patient has been untreated for many years, in which case there may be a degree of fixed obstruction).

PEF rate
Correlates well with spirometry and is simple to use, however effort dependent, doesn't measure lung volume, and only measures obstruction in large airways whereas spirometry looks at all airways.

Other
CXR.
FBC.
Allergy testing.
Sputum/ nasal smear for eosinophils if allergy suspected.

MANAGEMENT

Management of acute attack

First assess severity

Mild to moderate
1 - Oxygen at 6L/ min.
2 - Nebulised salbutamol (1-6 yrs - 2.5mg; 6 yrs to adult - 5mg).
3 - Repeat if required.
4 - Consider admission.

Severe attack
1. Oxygen at 10L/min.
- maintain sats >92%
2. Nebulised salbutamol (5mg if >6yrs; 2.5mg if < 6 yrs).
--> repeat if not effective x2 then every 15mins
--> consider adding ipratropium 0.5mg
3 - Transport promptly to hospital
4 - Insert an IV cannula.
5 - Give hydrocortisone 250mg IV (or prednisalone 30-60mg po) (adult)

Life threatening
1 - Admit
2 - Oxygen at 10L/min by mask or bag-mask ventilation.
- want sats >92.
3 - Nebulised salbutamol (O2 driven)
- adults - 5mg
- children - 2.5mg.
--> repeat if no response after 15mins
--> add ipratropium 0.5mg
4. Salbutamol IV
- 5mcg/kg over 2-5mins.
- if no response, 5-20mcg/min titrated to response (1-5mcg/kg/hr)
- start at 5, adjust accordingly.
3 - Adrenaline (controversial - if unable to breathe to take spacer/neb)
- adults 0.5mg IM (0.5ml 1:1000);
- children - 0.01ml of 1:1000 / kg IM.
- controversial to repeat doses at 1 min intervals (max 5 doses in kids) until improved.
4 - Insert an IV cannula.
5 - Hydrocortisone 200mg IV.
- repeat 6-hrly for first 24hrs.
6 - Measure potassium
- esp if using adrenaline
6 - CPR if necessary

Not Improving?
1. Aminophylline 5mg/kg IV loading dose over 30m.
2. Infusion aminophylline 0.5-0.7 mg/kg/hr.
- monitor dose, caution in combo with beta agonists (arrhythmia risk)
3. Neb saline at 15 mins intervals / continuous.
4. CXR - consider pneumothorax.

Monitoring
1.  Repeat PFR every 15mins
2.  Maintain sats >92
3.  Rpt ABGs if PaO2 was <60 and/or PCO2>45 or deteriorating.

Longer-term management

Conservative
Stop smoking and avoid inciting allergens.

Medical
Bronchodilators
Mild or infrequent asthma -> start with a short acting inhaled beta agonist (ventolin, terbutoline) or anticholinergic as needed (prn).
Inhaled steroids
Daily symptoms -> add low dose inhaled corticosteroids (200-400ug twice daily or can try nedocromil first if child).
Patients should be advised that these have no immediate symptom relief, but work after weeks, and must be taken regularly.
Gargling after inhaling prevents oral candidiasis.
If needed, move from low to high dose inhaled corticosteroids.
Decreases exacerbations but associated with more side effects.
Long acting beta-agonists
Can add a long acting inhaled beta agonist instead.
-> better symptom relief, but doesn't decrease exacerbations.
Second line in NZ - only if high-dose inhaled steroids don't work.
Theophylline
If neither of the options in work, add oral theophylline.

Assessment of control
Under control
No cough or wheeze at night (trough in medication concentration).
Symptom-free most days, exercising freely.
Little use of reliever.
PLAN - continue preventer, ?reduce dose.
Getting worse
Night waking, daily symptoms, more SOB on exercise.
Increased use of reliever.
Getting a cold.
PLAN - start or increase dose of preventer, use reliever as required.
Asthma severe
Breathless or wheezy despite increased preventer.
Reliever only lasting 2-3 hours.
PLAN - start prednisone, see doctor
Emergency
Difficulty speaking, feeling faint or frightened, reliever not working.
PLAN - acute management above.
