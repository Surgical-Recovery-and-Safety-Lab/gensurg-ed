Principles of Diagnosis of Acute Abdo Pain
Zachary Cope
1.  Make a serious and thorough attempt to diagnose predominantly be means of history and examination.

2.  The deduction and induction from observed facts necessary for the formation of a definite opinion provide good mental discipline for the observer, help to imprint upon the tables of the mind perceptions and clinical pictures that can usefully be recalled in the future.

3.  Diagnose early.  A responsible surgeon should evaluate the patient at the earliest possible time.

4.  A recent prospective randomised control trial has shown early narcotic administration does not obscure the correct diagnosis or delay inappropriate treatment.

5.  It is a curious but well known fact that many who develop abdominal pain in the daytime endure until evening before they feel compelled to come to hospital.  It follows that important decisions often have to be made at night when physicians, weary with the day's work, and with perceptions and reasoning faculties somewhat jaded, are both physically and mentally below their best.  Do not temporise!

6.  Severe abdo pain >6hrs is usually caused by conditions of surgical importance.

7.  The recovery rate from acute abdominal disease increases in proportion to the earliness of diagnosis and treatment.

8.  More early cases will be diagnosed by palpating the pelvic peritoneum than by perusing the CT scan.

9.  The subtleties of early diagnosis often lie in applied anatomy: the psoas stretch test showing early irritibility of that muscle, or the obturator internus test (rotating the flexed thigh inward to extreme).

10.  Failure to remember that pelvic peritonitis will not cause abdo wall rigidity leads to errors in diagnosis.  The pelvis and central part of posterior abdominal wall are 'nondemonstrative' areas, with much more subtle clinical manifestations than elsewhere.

11.  Pain is common from tubes whose walls are composed of smooth muscle fibers.  They have no high-grade sensibiilty, and not necessarily tender to palpate.  Intestine may be crushed, cut or teared, without any pain, yet stretching or distension will cause major pain.

12.  Tenderness due to irritation of nerves by a unilateral lesion is not felt on the opposite side.  For example, pleurisy on the right may cause tenderness and rigidity on the right, but not left iliac fossa.

13.  Shock onset within minutes or an hour or two of onset of abdo pain usually means haemorrhage.  Shock occuring in later stages of intra-abdominal catastrophes is a result of a loss of intravascular volume: v/d, sequestration into distended intestine, into abdo cavity, or bleeding into an infarcted intestine.

14.  Referred and localising visceral pain: eg the gallbladder.  nterior inflamed, irritates mucosa, causing mucus secretion, distension and central epigastric pain.  There may, however, be no pain on palpation of the bag, until bacterial invasion of the wall and localisation to irritation of parietal peritoneal nerves occur.

15.  Adrenal steroids may dampen localised or generalised peritonitis without usual signs and symptoms of inflammation sufficient to cause alarm.  Even perf'd DU, which 'rather inconclusive' evidence suggests are more common in steroid users.  Steroids supress fever.  Long term corticosteroid use can cause bizarre acute perforations of any part of the intestine.  Unknown why.  More often in collagen diseases.  Attenuation of abdo pain in pts being treated by corticosteroids makes it imperative to consider even slight abdo pain as serious.  Serious results may otherwise occur.  Exploration may be required sooner.

16.  It is always necessary to exclude medical diseases.  Interstitial nephritis, arteriosclerosis, cardiac derangements, tuberculosis, etc.  "It is regretful to have to say after laparotomy that there was severe albuminuria or that the lungs were not examined".

17.  Antibiotics may diminish the smptoms of ensuing peritonitis.  Take care of using them before diagnosis, and take account for their effects.
